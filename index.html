Fools Gold Analysis: Copper

What drives the price of copper, and can we project it?

Overview Copper Watch Drivers Strength Index Projections Findings

Objective

Identify what drives the price of copper, build projections with several methods (including a news-based strength index), and test whether they beat simple benchmarks such as a random walk and the futures curve.

What moves copper

  • Supply: mine output is concentrated in Chile, Peru, the DRC and China; new mines take 10+ years; strikes, accidents and export policy cause disruptions.
  • Demand: China uses about half the world's copper (construction, the power grid, manufacturing); electrification (EVs, renewables, data centers) is the long-term story.
  • Macro & financial: the US dollar (copper is priced in dollars), global growth, interest rates and speculative positioning.
  • Inventories: LME, COMEX and Shanghai stocks show how tight the physical market is.
  • Cost curve: the cash cost of the highest-cost producers acts as a soft floor over the long run.

Methods

  1. Market-implied: the futures curve as the market's own forecast (theory of storage).
  2. Time series: ARIMA on log prices, compared with a random walk.
  3. Driver regression: copper returns on the dollar, China activity, rates, inventories and the strength index.
  4. Monte Carlo: simulated paths from historical return and volatility, baseline and strength-index-adjusted.
  5. Benchmark: published bank and World Bank forecasts.

Phases

1Drivers

Learn the supply, demand, macro and inventory drivers.

2Data

Price history plus the dollar, China, rates and inventories.

3Explore

Chart history, returns, stationarity, correlations.

4Index

Check scoring rules, review past headlines, test lead vs lag.

5Projections

Futures curve, ARIMA, regression, Monte Carlo.

6Evaluate

Backtest, log projections, regress results vs actuals.

Backtest rule: use only information available on each date. Hindsight leaks in easily with news-driven prices.

News Strength Index

Card counting on copper headlines. Every headline is scored by its likely price impact: +1 (bullish for copper, e.g. a mine strike or China stimulus), 0 or −1 (bearish, e.g. new supply or a stronger dollar), by keyword rules (off-topic stories are ignored), and the scores are added up into a running total that fades over time: each score counts fully on its day and loses half its weight every 14 days. Scores can be overridden by hand.
